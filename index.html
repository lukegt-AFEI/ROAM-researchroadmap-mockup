About this roadmap

Arctic rewilding has a bold hypothesis behind it, one decades-long experiment at Pleistocene Park, and a scattered literature. What the field lacks is a shared account of what is known, what is contested, and what evidence would settle the question. This roadmap is a proposal for building that evidence, in order, with an honest price tag.

Arctic rewilding is not a silver bullet, and not a substitute for cutting emissions. Its effects would build over decades, broadly on the pace and scale of other carbon-removal approaches, and could offset a meaningful share of warming if the evidence holds up. Its distinctive promise is protecting permafrost carbon that emissions cuts can’t reach, alongside decarbonization and carbon removal, not instead of them.

It works backward from a single decision: whether large-herbivore restoration should be scaled up as a nature-based climate solution. An activity belongs on the roadmap only if it reduces an uncertainty that matters to that decision, or is a necessary step toward work that does. A clear “no” would be a valuable outcome.

How it is organized

The structure is adapted from Reflective’s SAI Research Roadmap, which borrows the phase-and-gate logic of clinical trials. The work runs in four phases. Phase 0 gathers and tests existing evidence and designs decisive experiments. Phase 1 runs controlled, replicated experiments at several sites in contrasting Arctic landscapes. Wild releases are not new: muskox and wood bison have been reintroduced for decades, but herds are growing far too slowly to matter. Phase 2 accelerates that work in several landscapes at once and at higher densities, while herds stay small enough to reverse. Phase 3 is the decision about scale: whether herds grow on their own, are pushed toward millions of animals, or are capped.

Because herds released as small founder groups grow exponentially, they grow slowly for decades. That gives a window of roughly 40 years in which growth can be held steady, slowed or reversed while effects are measured at landscape scale. Every five years, outcomes are compared with projections and triggers agreed before release decide whether to continue, pause, reverse or end the deployment.

Between phases are gates. Moving on is never automatic: the full body of evidence is weighed against criteria agreed in advance. ROAM proposes draft criteria, but the final criteria and the judgment of whether a gate has been met should belong to an independent scientific steering committee. Some activities, such as community engagement, monitoring and governance, continue through the gates.

Activities are grouped into four research tracks: ecosystem dynamics, climate outcomes, measurement and modeling, and implementation. Unlike some technical roadmaps, implementation includes engagement with Alaska Native organizations, Tribes, landholders and agencies, because research on shared land and wildlife can’t proceed without them.

Where the content comes from

The uncertainties follow the causal chains defined in the systematic literature review: herbivores to vegetation and carrying capacity; to snow, soil temperature and permafrost; to soil moisture and methane; to above- and below-ground carbon; and to albedo. Activities draw on ROAM’s planning documents, the Istanbul advisors’ meeting, and existing proposals.

Two uncertainties come from the sensitivity analysis in ROAM’s Arctic rewilding model rather than the review’s causal chains. Grazing that strips moss and shrubs from ice-rich permafrost could start thaw instead of preventing it, and this is one of the largest drivers of the model’s 2100 result. In the model, boreal land opens to grassland only after fire, and the forest carbon given up where burned stands are held open offsets about half the modeled cooling by 2100. That is a limitation of the model: much of the boreal zone is shrubland, wetland and open woodland where animals could do well without fire, so the roadmap treats boreal transition pathways as an open question.

Every cost and timeline here is a placeholder. The next step is to scope each activity with outside experts, cross-check their estimates, and replace these numbers with ranges the field can stand behind.

How the estimates are built

Each activity has a minimum scope, what it would take to answer its question at all, and an extended scope that would make the answer more robust. Once activities are scoped with outside experts, each cost estimate should cover personnel, equipment, supplies, animal care, travel to field sites, data and compute, and an indirect cost rate, without inflation. Durations run from the start of an activity to a preprint, since results are usually usable before formal publication.

A phase lasts as long as its longest chain of dependent activities. Work further in the future is less certain, so later estimates are marked low-confidence, and open-ended work is marked “ongoing” or “not yet scoped.” Deployment costs are shown for the first decade only.

One headline metric

To show what each phase buys, the roadmap will track one quantity: the uncertainty in global cooling impact, meaning the change in global mean temperature that Arctic rewilding could deliver at scale, combining permafrost, carbon, methane, albedo and fire effects across the area herds could plausibly occupy. It is poorly constrained today, it depends on several other uncertainties (carrying capacity, the snow effect, soil carbon, grazing-triggered thaw, the boreal fire pathway), and it is what any decision to scale up is ultimately about.

Two large drivers of that range can’t be narrowed by rewilding research: climate sensitivity, meaning how much the planet warms or cools per unit of radiative forcing, and which warming scenario the world follows. In ROAM’s Arctic rewilding model, climate sensitivity is the largest single driver of the 2100 result and the warming scenario is among the larger ones. The roadmap therefore reports them separately. Cooling is stated under a named warming scenario with climate sensitivity held at a central value, and each phase’s uncertainty reduction counts only the rewilding-specific inputs its experiments would narrow. Today that range can be estimated from the spread across the back-of-the-envelope and geospatial models. Those numbers haven’t been calculated yet, so the phase headers say “not yet estimated.”

A single cooling number leaves out uncertainties that matter just as much to a decision: effects on biodiversity, other wildlife, fire and hydrology; effects on communities, subsistence, food security and Indigenous rights; animal welfare; and how governance would work across jurisdictions. These aren’t reduced to a figure here. They’re tracked alongside the climate questions in the uncertainty view, and they weigh on every gate and deployment review.

Integrity commitments

ROAM both convenes this field and plans to run an experimental site in it. To keep those roles separate, results from ROAM’s own site will go through independent review before they carry weight in gate decisions. Site data will be open on published terms, outside researchers will be able to work at the site, and ROAM will say which role it is speaking in.
